7 Days on the Same Side

Your child loves card games. In seven days, learn to turn those games into ten minutes a day of real connection, and handle the moments that usually end in tears: losing, rows over the rules, and wanting more cards.

Day 1 · Start with you

Regulate yourself first

Your child can't borrow calm you don't have. Before any technique works on them, it has to work on you.

The 30-second version

The moment: When a bad draw (or a bad day) winds you up

The one thing: Name your feeling out loud, then take three slow breaths before you answer.

Tonight: Write down three warning signs that come before you snap.

💡The idea

When stress hits, the thinking part of the brain goes offline and the alarm system takes over. Psychiatrist Daniel Siegel calls this "flipping your lid". It happens to children constantly — and to dads more often than we'd like to admit.

The fastest way back is to notice it and put a word to it. Siegel's phrase for this is "name it to tame it": saying "I'm getting frustrated" out loud actually turns the volume down. Kids learn to do this by watching you do it first.

From The Whole-Brain Child by Daniel J. Siegel and Tina Payne Bryson.

💬Try saying

I'm feeling a bit wound up. I'm going to take three slow breaths before I answer. Give me a minute — I want to be calm when we talk about this.

🃏At the card table

Card games are full of tiny frustrations: a bad draw, a forgotten rule, a lost game. Use them. When it's your bad luck, say what you feel out loud — "Oh, that's annoying, I really needed that card" — then take a breath and carry on. You're showing your child exactly what calming down looks like.

10-minute challenge

Find your warning signs

Think back to the last time you lost your temper with your child. Write down three things that were happening before you snapped — in your body (jaw, shoulders, heart rate) and in your day (tired, hungry, rushed). Those are your early warnings. Next time you notice one, pause and breathe before you speak.

👧By age

Under 7Young children read your face and voice more than your words. Keep it short: "Daddy needs a big breath," then do it where they can see.
7 to 12Explain what you're doing and why, then invite them to try the three breaths with you. They'll start using it themselves.

What's one situation that reliably winds you up — and what could you do in the first ten seconds instead?

🧱 Take it off the table

Check it: “Which kind of cross am I: tired, worried or treated unfairly?”

Name it: Calm comes before any technique works.

Find it: the same skill shows up in traffic, a work email, a sibling row, a bad day at school.

This week: Notice one moment away from the cards when you, or your child, took a breath before reacting.

Day 2 · Start with you

Become the dad you needed

Most of us parent the way we were parented — until we decide on purpose to do something different.

The 30-second version

The moment: When you want to be the dad they'll remember

The one thing: Let your child see you enjoy them: catch one clever play and say so.

Tonight: Picture your ideal dad, and give your child one of his qualities today.

💡The idea

The Ideal Parent Protocol was developed by psychologists Daniel P. Brown and David S. Elliott. It's a guided imagery method: you picture parent figures who give you exactly what a child needs. They describe five qualities that help a child feel secure:

  1. Safety — "You're protected. I've got you."
  2. Attunement — really seeing what's going on inside your child.
  3. Soothing — comfort and reassurance when things go wrong.
  4. Delight — your child can see you enjoy them, just as they are.
  5. Encouragement — backing them to be their best self.

Today isn't therapy. It's simpler: use those five qualities as a picture of the dad you want to be.

From Attachment Disturbances in Adults by Daniel P. Brown and David S. Elliott. The full protocol is usually done with a trained therapist — if today brings up something heavy about your own childhood, that's a good reason to talk to one.

💬Try saying

I love watching you play. You get so into it. Whatever happens in this game, I'm on your side.

🃏At the card table

Delight is the quality dads most often forget to show. During your next game, catch your child doing something clever and say so — "Ooh, nice play, I didn't see that coming." Let your face show you're enjoying them, not just the game.

10-minute challenge

Picture your ideal dad

Sit somewhere quiet. Close your eyes and imagine a dad who gives a child all five qualities above. How does he speak? What does his face do when his child walks in? Then pick the one quality that came least naturally to you growing up, and look for one chance to give it to your child today.

👧By age

Under 7Delight is mostly physical at this age: a big smile when they walk in, a hug, a happy voice. They feel it before they understand it.
7 to 12Older children notice when you remember what matters to them. Ask about their favourite card or character and listen to the answer.

Which of the five qualities did you get plenty of as a child, and which did you miss?

🧱 Take it off the table

Check it: “Which kind of parent did I need just then?”

Name it: My child needs to feel safe, seen, soothed and delighted in.

Find it: the same skill shows up in how you react to mistakes, at bedtime, after school.

This week: Give one of the five qualities at a moment that has nothing to do with cards.

Day 3 · Build the bond

Play and connection

Play is a child's first language. When you play their way, they start telling you things.

The 30-second version

The moment: When they ask you to play

The one thing: Say yes, put your phone in another room, and let them make the rules.

Tonight: Ten minutes, their game, their rules, no correcting.

💡The idea

Psychologist Lawrence J. Cohen argues that play is how children build closeness, work through worries and regain confidence. The magic ingredients are simple: follow their lead, be a bit silly, and let them win at things they usually lose at.

Even ten minutes of undivided attention — phone in another room, no multitasking — counts for more than an hour of half-there time.

From Playful Parenting by Lawrence J. Cohen.

💬Try saying

You choose — what do you want to play? Will you teach me how this card works? I haven't figured it out.

🃏At the card table

Let your child be the expert. Ask them to teach you their favourite card game, or explain their deck to you. Being the one who knows more than Dad is a huge confidence boost — and kids talk most freely when their hands are busy. New to card games yourself? Our beginner's guide gets you going.

10-minute challenge

Ten minutes, their rules

Set a timer for ten minutes. Phone out of the room. Your child picks the game and makes the rules — you just join in with enthusiasm. No correcting, no teaching, no checking the time. When the timer goes, thank them for letting you play.

👧By age

Under 7Real rules don't matter yet. Let them sort cards by colour, invent rules or tell stories about the characters — that all counts as play.
7 to 12Let them teach you a real game, choose your deck and explain their strategy. Being the expert is the best part for them.

🧱 Take it off the table

Check it: “Which kind of time does my child want: talking, doing or just being near?”

Name it: Ten minutes of their lead, full attention, builds the bond.

Find it: the same skill shows up in a walk, cooking together, a car journey with the radio off.

This week: Do your ten minutes once without any cards at all.

Day 4 · Talk so they listen

Nonviolent Communication

How you say it decides whether your child hears a request or an attack.

The 30-second version

The moment: When there's a row over the rules

The one thing: Swap "you're doing it wrong" for what you saw, how you feel, and a clear ask.

Tonight: Rewrite the thing you nag about most, using the four steps.

💡The idea

Marshall Rosenberg's Nonviolent Communication swaps blame for four simple steps:

  1. Observation — what you actually saw, without judgement. "Your cards are all over the floor", not "you're so messy".
  2. Feeling — how you feel about it. "I feel frustrated…"
  3. Need — what matters to you underneath. "…because I need the room clear so nobody treads on them."
  4. Request — a clear, doable ask. "Would you put them back in the box before tea?"

It works the other way too: when your child is upset, try to guess the feeling and need behind their words.

From Nonviolent Communication: A Language of Life by Marshall B. Rosenberg.

💬Try saying

When the game ended with cards thrown, I felt sad, because I want this to be fun for both of us. Can we try again and shake hands at the end? Are you feeling frustrated because you really wanted to win that one?

🃏At the card table

Disagreements over rules are perfect practice. Instead of "No, you're doing it wrong", try an observation and a request: "I read that card differently. Shall we look up the rule together?" You stay on the same team, even when you disagree.

10-minute challenge

Rewrite one nag

Think of something you say to your child most days — the shoes, the screens, the bedtime. Rewrite it using the four steps: observation, feeling, need, request. Say the new version today, and notice whether the response is different.

👧By age

Under 7Keep it to a feeling and a request: "I feel worried when you climb on that. Please come down." Four steps is too many for little ones.
7 to 12Use all four steps, then teach them the pattern and let them try it on you. You may hear a lot more about what they need.

What's the need underneath the thing you nag about most?

🧱 Take it off the table

Check it: “Which kind of request is this: one they can say no to, or a demand?”

Name it: Say what you saw, how you feel, what you need, then ask clearly.

Find it: the same skill shows up in homework, bedtime, a request to a partner or a colleague.

This week: Use the four steps once away from the table, with anyone.

Day 5 · Big feelings

Emotion coaching

Big feelings aren't a problem to shut down. They're your best chance to get closer.

The 30-second version

The moment: When they lose

The one thing: Validate first ("that really stung"), and solve the problem later.

Tonight: Play one game and name every feeling together.

💡The idea

Psychologist John Gottman studied how parents respond to children's emotions. The parents whose kids did best followed five steps he calls emotion coaching:

  1. Notice the emotion, ideally while it's still small.
  2. See it as a chance to connect and teach, not an inconvenience.
  3. Listen and validate — let them know the feeling makes sense.
  4. Help them name it — frustrated, disappointed, embarrassed.
  5. Set limits and solve problems together — all feelings are OK, not all behaviour is.

From Raising An Emotionally Intelligent Child by John Gottman.

💬Try saying

That loss really stung, didn't it? It makes sense to feel gutted. It's OK to be angry. It's not OK to throw cards. What could you do instead next time?

🃏At the card table

Losing a game is a safe, low-stakes rehearsal for disappointment. When your child loses, resist "It's only a game". Validate first — "You were so close, that's frustrating" — and only once they've calmed down, ask what they might try differently next game.

10-minute challenge

Feelings detective

Play one game together. Each time something exciting or annoying happens, both of you name the feeling — the more precise the better ("gutted", "smug", "nervous"). It sounds silly, but you're building your child's emotional vocabulary, and it gives them words to use when things get hard.

👧By age

Under 7Offer simple words — happy, sad, cross, scared — and point to faces in a book or on a card to help them match the feeling.
7 to 12Go for richer words like disappointed, embarrassed or jealous, and solve the problem together once they're calm.

Which emotion in your child do you find hardest to sit with — and why might that be?

🧱 Take it off the table

Check it: “Which kind of feeling is this, exactly?”

Name it: Big feelings are a chance to get closer, not a problem to shut down.

Find it: the same skill shows up in a friendship fall-out, nerves before school, a pet being poorly.

This week: Name one feeling together that came from outside the game.

Day 6 · Hold the line

Boundaries without punishment

Being kind and being firm aren't opposites. Great dads do both at the same time.

The 30-second version

The moment: When they want more cards

The one thing: Be kind and firm at the same time: warm voice, same answer.

Tonight: Agree three house rules for spending, trading and packing away.

💡The idea

After Day 4 and Day 5, some dads wonder whether they're still allowed to say no. You are. Jane Nelsen's Positive Discipline is built on being kind and firm at the same time: warm in how you say it, consistent in sticking to it.

Instead of punishments that come out of nowhere, agree the rules in advance and use consequences that are related to the problem, respectful and reasonable. Kids follow rules they helped make far more readily than rules handed down.

From Positive Discipline by Jane Nelsen.

💬Try saying

I love playing with you, and the answer is still no. We'll play again tomorrow. The rule we agreed was no trading without asking first. So this one goes back until we've talked about it.

🃏At the card table

Card games come with natural boundaries: how much to spend, who you trade with, when game time ends. Set them together before they become a fight. A simple house rule like "no trading without a parent's OK" protects your child from bad trades and keeps you in the loop without being the bad guy.

10-minute challenge

Make three house rules together

Sit down with your child and agree three rules for card games at home — for example, spending, trading and when to pack away. Agree what happens if a rule is broken, write them down and stick them on the fridge. Your child gets a say; you get the final word.

👧By age

Under 7One or two simple rules is plenty, with a consequence that happens straight away, like the game being put away for now.
7 to 12Let them help write the rules and the consequences, and agree to review them together in a month.

Do you tend to lean too soft or too strict — and what would "kind and firm" look like for you?

🧱 Take it off the table

Check it: “Which kind of no is this: a firm one, or one that's open to discussion?”

Name it: Kind and firm at the same time.

Find it: the same skill shows up in screen time, sweets, bedtime, spending.

This week: Hold one warm, firm no about something other than cards.

Day 7 · When it goes wrong

Repair after rupture

You will get it wrong. Super dads aren't the ones who never slip — they're the ones who come back and fix it.

The 30-second version

The moment: When a game night goes wrong

The one thing: Come back, own your part, say sorry, then play.

Tonight: Make one repair, then spend ten minutes on something they love.

💡The idea

Daniel Siegel and Mary Hartzell describe moments where the connection between parent and child breaks — shouting, dismissing, losing it — as ruptures. They're inevitable. What matters is the repair: coming back, owning your part, and reconnecting.

A good repair teaches your child that relationships survive mistakes, that grown-ups take responsibility, and that they weren't to blame for your temper.

From Parenting from the Inside Out by Daniel J. Siegel and Mary Hartzell.

💬Try saying

Earlier I shouted at you. That wasn't OK, and it wasn't your fault. I'm sorry. I was stressed and I took it out on you. Next time I'm going to take a breath first. Can we start again?

🃏At the card table

A game is a brilliant way to reconnect after a rough moment, because it gives you something to do side by side. After you've apologised, try "Fancy a game?" You don't need to talk it all through — playing together is the repair.

10-minute challenge

Make one repair

Think of a moment this week — or longer ago — when you got it wrong with your child. Go back to it. Name what happened, own your part without "but", say sorry, and say what you'll do differently. Then spend ten minutes doing something they love.

👧By age

Under 7Keep it short and simple — "I shouted, and I'm sorry. I love you" — followed by a cuddle.
7 to 12Say more about what happened, ask how it felt for them, and listen without defending yourself.

Did anyone ever repair with you as a child? How might it have changed things if they had?
